We have started to settle on how we hand context to an agent. Instruction files at the repository root, skills, layered system prompts, a handful of memory approaches. It is a real maturing of the craft, and it is good at one specific thing: documenting what I will call **Prescriptive Context** - the rules and standing guidance an agent is told to follow.

But prescription only goes so far. In practice I keep stepping back into the loop. To ask the right question. To nudge the agent down a different path. To supply a hard-won insight, or point it at the right tool at the right moment. That accumulated human experience is a different kind of context. It is less structured than the rules, and it is more the kind of _ah-ha_ moment that saves the day. I call it **Reflective Context**, and right now it is neither here nor there. We have very sophisticated memory containers, but I believe there is a heap more clarity to surface in how we hook them up.

## The problem: context is smart but lacks experience

The ecosystem has stabilised on a well-described structure for _prescriptive_ rules. We also worked out early that an agent needs memory - or in some cases something closer to fine-tuning through context. Those are the RAG and vector stores, the LLM wiki, the MCP data sources.

I am not here to challenge that we have those things. What I want to challenge is **the way we currently reach for them, and the moment at which we do**. We tend to load memory the way we load everything else - up front, indiscriminately, as part of the standing context. That is the wrong time and the wrong shape for experience. Experience is precedent. It only matters when the rules in front of you ran out.

## The Legal Framework

The Legal Framework provides a hierarchy to structure the context, but more importantly it describes the role each layer plays at runtime. The three layers are: **ideology** (the Constitution), **prescription** (the Legislation), and **reflection** (the Common Law). The goal is to deliver the appropriate dimension of context just-in-time, and the flow-on effect is a more concise, more manageable set of Legislation and Constitution.

### The Constitution

The Constitution is the root instruction file — CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md, or your harness's equivalent — written as a concise set of points governing the values needed to work successfully on the repository. It is highly likely to be global, or at least easily transferable across projects, because constitutional values are about _how to behave as a developer_, not about any one codebase. [Karpathy's published CLAUDE.md](#) is a strong exemplar: four behavioural pillars — think before coding, simplicity first, surgical changes, and goal-driven execution with verifiable success criteria — in about sixty lines.

The Framework treats the Constitution as the guiding north-star, and it is always our final fallback when handling ambiguity.

### Legislation

Legislation is the prescriptive application of the Constitution. It is where we describe specific architecture, design patterns, workflows, and tool references. At runtime we rely on the just-in-time loading of context relevant to each sub-directory.

The Framework doesn't really differ from the majority here - Legislation is rightly the guardrails. What it does present is a scenario where the guardrails can be reduced to a more maintainable size and update frequency.

### Common Law

Common Law is the accumulated experience of the developer, ideally chunked into useful facts: points of verification, the issues we have encountered, our fixes, our preferences, and examples of what to do and what not to do. In short, they are actual interpretations of the Legislation, providing the nuance that strengthens and positively narrows a legislative piece.

The Framework requires the Common Law to meet the runtime requirement too, by hooking into a new concept - the Ambiguity Event. Guided by the legal system as precedent, Common Law is applied only when the Legislation has not been clear enough. The Ambiguity Event is raised as the optimal moment to reference it. How it is referenced is also heavily borrowed from our legal system: we invoke the Common Law as Memory, since we are seeking guidance from past work.

On a more technical level, I believe this is only a small adjustment, given we already have the Memory.md file being accessed.

## The Mechanism: Recall at the Point of Ambiguity

The Framework also requires some tuning to the core harness. The goal is to build a stronger sense of uncertainty into its thinking, and then give it the tools to work off our experience.

    its Constitutional AI harness.

</aside>

To capture ambiguity even in the face of false confidence, we raise a new concept: the Clarity Assessment. The Clarity Assessment is a request for the model to reason by linting its action against the Legislation and raising a scored list of the assumptions it has made. The reason for a scored list, and not just a list, is pragmatism - we want to let a developer determine the model's level of automation, rather than automatically flooding our tokens with every possible assumption.

Once we have the list of assumptions that need guidance, we can make the request to our Common Law. Ideally this context layer is wrapped by a strongly typed request structure, to promote a format that minimises reasoning. For each assumption, the model breaks down into tag values what it is seeking - verification, issue, fix, preference or example - plus keywords. The combination runs a search across an ideally graphified, or simply weighted, collection and returns the top 3 weighted context snippets most relevant to the tags.

## The Feedback Loop: Legislation Review

As with most good developer systems, we want to acknowledge and cater for a feedback loop. Here, we want it so we can tune our Legislation and Constitution to minimise the need for Common Law - for the sake of efficient reasoning. This doesn't mean we continuously pull context in from the Common Law and make it prescriptive. Rather, it gives us a channel to tune the wording already in use, so it better matches our intent - the intent that led to an assumption being made in the first place. In more extreme cases we may identify a shift in pattern, and then we may be able to change Legislation materially.

## In Summary

In summary, the Legal Framework is first and foremost a way to frame context under different lights, so that we can better see how and when to use each. I lean into the legal framing quite deliberately, because so far I have seen it as the _perfect_ fit for what context is really doing - and I think we, as humans, have basically solved context in the Legal System. The follow-up point is that we should adjust our current harnesses to align more with how we can hook in the different layers of context. This is where I think we will see big wins: both in conveying our message accurately to the models, and in more efficient use of our tokens. I may have understated it in the framework, but the timing of the context snippets also plays on the weighting of where the context sits within the context window - so with fewer words, we should be able to garner better outcomes.
