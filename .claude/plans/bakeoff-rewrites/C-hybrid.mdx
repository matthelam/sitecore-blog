We have started to settle on how we hand context to an agent. Instruction files at the repository root, skills, layered system prompts, a handful of memory approaches. It is a real maturing of the craft, and it is good at one thing: documenting what I will call **Prescriptive Context** - the rules and standing guidance an agent is told to follow.

Prescription only goes so far. In practice I keep stepping back into the loop - to ask the right question, to nudge the agent down a different path, to supply a hard-won insight or point it at the right tool at the right moment. That accumulated human experience is a different kind of context: less structured than the rules, more the _ah-ha_ moment that saves the day. I call it **Reflective Context**, and right now it is neither here nor there. We have very sophisticated memory containers, but I believe there is a heap more clarity to surface in how we hook them up.

## The problem: context is smart but lacks experience

The ecosystem has stabilised on a well-described structure for _prescriptive_ rules. We also worked out early that an agent needs memory - or something closer to fine-tuning through context. Those are the RAG and vector stores, the LLM wiki, the MCP data sources.

I am not challenging that we have those things. I am challenging **the way we reach for them, and the moment at which we do**. We load memory the way we load everything else - up front, indiscriminately, as part of the standing context. That is the wrong time and the wrong shape for experience. **Experience is precedent. It only matters when the rules in front of you ran out.**

## The Legal Framework

The Legal Framework gives context a hierarchy, but more importantly it names the role each layer plays at runtime. Three layers: **ideology** (the Constitution), **prescription** (the Legislation), and **reflection** (the Common Law). Deliver the right dimension of context just-in-time, and the flow-on effect is a leaner, more manageable Legislation and Constitution.

### The Constitution

The Constitution is the root instruction file — CLAUDE.md, AGENTS.md, or your harness's equivalent — a concise set of values for working successfully on the repository. It is highly likely to be global, or at least easily transferable across projects, because constitutional values are about _how to behave as a developer_, not about any one codebase. [Karpathy's published CLAUDE.md](#) is a strong exemplar: four behavioural pillars — think before coding, simplicity first, surgical changes, and goal-driven execution with verifiable success criteria — in about sixty lines.

The Framework treats the Constitution as the guiding north-star, and it is always our final fallback when handling ambiguity.

### Legislation

Legislation is the prescriptive application of the Constitution: specific architecture, design patterns, workflows, and tool references. At runtime we rely on just-in-time loading, scoped to each sub-directory.

The Framework doesn't differ from the majority here - Legislation is rightly the guardrails. What it adds is a scenario where those guardrails can shrink to a more maintainable size and update frequency.

### Common Law

Common Law is the accumulated experience of the developer, chunked into useful facts: points of verification, the issues we have hit, our fixes, our preferences, and examples of what to do and what not to do. They are actual interpretations of the Legislation - the nuance that strengthens and positively narrows a legislative piece.

The Framework holds the Common Law to the same runtime requirement, by hooking into a new concept - the Ambiguity Event. Guided by the legal system as precedent, **Common Law is applied only when the Legislation has not been clear enough.** The Ambiguity Event marks that moment. How we reference it is also borrowed from the legal system: we invoke the Common Law as Memory, because we are seeking guidance from past work.

Technically, I believe this is a small adjustment, given we already access the Memory.md file.

## The Mechanism: Recall at the Point of Ambiguity

The Framework also needs some tuning to the core harness. The goal: build a stronger sense of uncertainty into its thinking, then give it the tools to work off our experience.

    its Constitutional AI harness.
  </p>
</aside>

To capture ambiguity even in the face of false confidence, we raise a new concept: the Clarity Assessment. The Clarity Assessment asks the model to lint its action against the Legislation and raise a scored list of the assumptions it has made. The list is scored, not flat, for a pragmatic reason - we want the developer to set the model's level of automation, not flood our tokens with every possible assumption.

With the list of assumptions that need guidance, we query our Common Law. Ideally this layer is wrapped in a strongly typed request structure, to promote a format that minimises reasoning. For each assumption, the model names in tag values what it is seeking - verification, issue, fix, preference, or example - plus keywords. That combination searches an ideally graphified, or simply weighted, collection and returns the top 3 weighted snippets most relevant to the tags.

## The Feedback Loop: Legislation Review

Like most good developer systems, this one caters for a feedback loop. Here it exists to tune our Legislation and Constitution so we need the Common Law less, for the sake of efficient reasoning. This doesn't mean continuously pulling context out of the Common Law and making it prescriptive. It gives us a channel to tune the wording already in use, so it better matches our intent - the intent that led to the assumption in the first place. In more extreme cases we may identify a shift in pattern, and then change Legislation materially.

## In Summary

The Legal Framework is first and foremost a way to frame context under different lights, so we can see how and when to use each. I lean into the legal framing deliberately, because so far I have seen it as the _perfect_ fit for what context is really doing - and I think we, as humans, have basically solved context in the Legal System. The follow-on: we should adjust our current harnesses to align with how we hook in the different layers of context. That is where I expect the big wins - both in conveying our message accurately to the models, and in more efficient use of our tokens. I may have understated this in the framework, but the timing of a snippet also shifts its weight within the context window - so with fewer words, we should garner better outcomes.
