# Blog Inception — the creative stage

A post is made in two stages. **Inception** (this skill) decides what the story is: the point, who
it's for, how it unfolds, and where it turns. The **technical write-up** comes later, once the
concept is locked, and turns the brief into wording, structure and visuals. Keep them apart.
When shaping and wording happen at once, the wording ends up papering over problems the shape
should have solved, and a post with no clear point gets polished instead of rethought.

## Who brings what

The author brings the topic, the substance, the evidence, the experience and the opinions. This
skill brings the questions that surface the point, the arc options, and the structure that makes
the substance land. It never invents a claim, a result, a number or an anecdote. Where the story
needs something the material doesn't contain, mark it **author to supply** and move on.

## Process

1. **Take in the raw material as given.** Notes, a transcript, one sentence, a pile of links. Don't
   tidy it yet; the mess often shows where the energy is.
2. **Find the point.** Offer two or three candidate one-liners drawn from the material: the
   sentence the reader should leave with. The author picks or rewrites. This line becomes the
   emphasis line the finished post returns to. If no point emerges, say so plainly. "There isn't a
   post here yet, and this is what's missing" is a useful inception outcome.
3. **Find the reader.** One sentence each: who they are, what they believe now, and what should
   change for them by the end.
4. **Find the tension.** What's wrong, costly, surprising or unresolved: the reason anyone reads
   past the first paragraph. A post without tension is a reference page.
5. **Choose the shape and size honestly.** Size follows the evidence the author actually has: a
   single-point tip, a short essay, a long essay, a step-by-step guide, or a series. Propose a
   series only when each part can stand on its own and answers one question.
6. **Propose two or three arcs** from `references/arc-patterns.md`, each written out as numbered
   beats. For every beat, state what it does for the reader and what evidence it needs. Recommend
   one and say why. Offering alternatives matters: the first arc that comes to mind is usually the
   most generic one.
7. **Mark the turning points.** Name the beats where the reader's model should change: the
   realisation, the reveal, the moment the cost becomes real. These are the visual moments. Say
   what the reader should *see or do* there ("feel the cost compound", "see the four repeats
   collapse into one"), not how to build it. Building is the later stage's job.
8. **Plan the emphasis.** Decide where the one-liner returns: usually the opening, a turn in the
   argument, and the close. Deliberate repetition of the point is part of the design.
9. **Plan the honesty beats.** What isn't solved, what the author got wrong first, prior art they
   found, what they'd do differently. This blog's credibility comes from these beats, so plan
   them in; don't leave them to chance.
10. **List the open questions and the evidence still needed.**
11. **Write the brief** using `assets/brief-template.md`, show it, and iterate. Mark it **Locked**,
    with the date, only when the author says so. A locked brief is the hand-off to the write-up
    stage.

## Where briefs live

Save each brief as `.claude/briefs/<working-slug>.md`. The slug can change when the post gets its
real title.

## What this skill doesn't do

- Write the post's body prose. Title options, candidate one-liners and candidate opening hooks are
  in scope, because finding them is creative work. Paragraphs are not.
- Invent evidence, examples, numbers or quotes.
- Choose the arc without offering alternatives, or lock a brief without the author.
- Decide tone, word-level style, components or file formats. Those belong to the write-up stage.
